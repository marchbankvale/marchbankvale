MV/N-017 · Leasehold

Standardised service charge demands are coming. Most blocks are not ready

1 September 2026 · 5 min read

Service charge arrears are rarely lost on the merits. They are lost on the form of the demand, and the form of the demand is about to change.

The service charge transparency measures under the leasehold reforms will introduce standardised demand forms, an annual report, standardised service charge accounts, and an administration charge schedule. Delivery is expected through at least five statutory instruments, anticipated later in 2026, with leaseholders seeing changes during 2027.

The lead-in periods have been indicated as twelve months for private landlords on the annual report, demand forms and standardised accounts, twenty-four months for social landlords, twelve months for the administration charge schedule and insurance transparency measures, and around three months for the litigation costs regulations.

Twelve months sounds generous. It is twelve months from regulations that have not yet been laid, to a change in the document on which every recovery depends.

Why the form of the demand decides the case

This is already the position. Ground rent is not payable until a demand in the prescribed form under section 166 of the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002 has been served. Service charges are subject to the eighteen-month limit in section 20B of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985. A demand that fails on either point does not create a weak debt. It creates no debt yet at all.

Standardised forms extend that logic. Once a prescribed form exists, using something else is not a presentational choice — it is a defect, and one a leaseholder's adviser will find immediately.

What management companies should be doing now

  • Establish which of your demands are already non-compliant. In our experience a meaningful proportion of ground rent demands would not survive a section 166 challenge today, before any reform.
  • Check every service charge item against the section 20B eighteen-month limit, and whether a notification of costs to come was served where the demand itself was not.
  • Identify where in your process the demand template is produced, because that is the thing that will have to change.
  • Note the litigation costs regulations separately. A three-month lead-in is not long enough to rebuild a process reactively.

The reforms are aimed at transparency for leaseholders and are likely to achieve it. The corollary for freeholders and management companies is that the margin for a sloppy demand, already thin, is closing.
